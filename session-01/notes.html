How LLMs Work

What happens inside ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude when you type a question? This session explains it in plain language. No heavy math.

1. The big picture

People use "AI", "ML", "deep learning" and "GenAI" as if they mean the same thing. They don't. Each one sits inside the one before it:

Artificial Intelligence (AI)Any machine that does something we'd call "smart". Includes old rule-based systems.
Machine Learning (ML)The machine learns patterns from examples instead of being given rules.
Deep Learning (DL)ML with large neural networks that have many layers.
Generative AI and LLMsDeep learning models that create new text, images, code or audio.

Traditional programming vs machine learning

Traditional programmingMachine learning
You give itRules + dataData + correct answers (examples)
It gives youAnswersThe rules (a "model")
Spam filter exampleif "lottery" in email: spam. Spammers write "l0ttery" and it breaks.Show it 10,000 emails labelled spam / not spam. It learns the patterns itself, including ones you never thought of.
Example: placement prediction Give an ML model past student records (CGPA, internships, projects, communication score) and whether each student got placed. It learns which factors matter and predicts the chance for a new student. Nobody wrote the rule "CGPA > 7.5 and 1 internship = likely placed". The model found it.

Deep learning

Deep learning uses neural networks: layers of simple maths units loosely inspired by brain neurons. "Deep" just means many layers. Each layer learns something a little more complex than the one before.

Example: face unlock on your phone Early layers spot edges and lines. Middle layers spot eyes, nose, mouth. Final layers recognise your face. No one programmed "what an eye looks like". The network learned it from millions of images.

2. Transformers and attention

In 2017, researchers at Google published a paper called "Attention Is All You Need". It introduced the transformer, the design behind almost every modern LLM. The "T" in GPT stands for Transformer.

Older models read a sentence one word at a time, left to right, and often forgot the start of long sentences. A transformer looks at all the words at once and works out which words matter for each other. This is called attention.

Example: the word "bank"

"I went to the bank to deposit my scholarship money." → attention links bank with deposit and money, so it means a financial bank.

"We sat on the river bank and watched the sunset." → attention links bank with river, so it means the side of a river.

Same word, different meaning, decided by the words around it.
Example: what does "it" mean? "The dog didn't cross the road because it was too tired." → it = the dog.
"The dog didn't cross the road because it was too busy." → it = the road.
You figured this out instantly. Attention is how a transformer does the same.

3. An LLM is a next-word predictor

A Large Language Model (LLM) is a huge transformer trained on a huge amount of text (books, websites, code, Wikipedia) to do one simple job: predict the next word.

Example Input: "Virat Kohli is famous for playing ___"
The model scores every possible next word: cricket 92%, for 3%, in 2%, ... and picks one. Then it adds that word to the input and predicts the next one again. Word by word, it writes a whole answer.
Key idea An LLM does not "look up" answers. It generates the most likely continuation of the text, one piece at a time. It's very good at this because it has seen so much text. Everything else in this workshop builds on this one idea.

"Large" refers to the number of parameters: the adjustable numbers inside the network that store what it learned. Modern LLMs have billions of them.

From predictor to helpful assistant

A model that only predicts the next word isn't a good chatbot yet. Ask it "What is the capital of France?" and it might continue with "What is the capital of Germany?" because that looks like a quiz list. So it is trained in stages:

1. Pre-trainingRead a huge part of the internet and learn to predict the next word. Like reading an entire library.
2. Instruction tuningLearn from thousands of example question → good answer pairs. Like learning to answer exam questions.
3. Human feedback (RLHF)Humans rank answers; the model learns to prefer helpful, honest, safe ones. Like a mentor giving feedback.

4. Tokens

LLMs don't read words or letters. They read tokens: small chunks of text. A token can be a whole word, part of a word, a space plus a word, or punctuation.

Example (illustrative split) "I love unbelievable biryani!" might become:
I love unbelievable biryani!
Common words are usually one token. Rare words get split into pieces. The exact split depends on each model's tokenizer.
Rule of thumb (English) 1 token ≈ 4 characters ≈ ¾ of a word. So 100 tokens ≈ 75 words, and a 2-page essay ≈ 1,000 tokens.

Why tokens matter

  • Cost: APIs charge per token, for both your input and the model's output.
  • Limits: every model has a maximum number of tokens it can handle at once (see context window).
  • Indian languages cost more: the same sentence in Hindi, Kannada or Tamil often takes several times more tokens than in English, because tokenizers saw less of that text in training. More tokens = more cost and less room.
  • Strange mistakes: ask a model "How many r's are in strawberry?" and older models often said 2. They see tokens like str aw berry, not individual letters.
Try it Paste a sentence into OpenAI Tokenizer or Tiktokenizer. Try the same sentence in English and in Hindi and compare the token count.

5. Embeddings

Computers work with numbers, not meaning. An embedding turns a word, sentence or document into a list of numbers (a vector) so that things with similar meaning end up close together.

Example: search by meaning, not keywords

Student asks: "How do I apply for placements?"

Sentence in the FAQSimilarity (illustrative)
What is the process for campus recruitment?0.82 (very close)
Steps to register with the training & placement cell0.78
Placement of furniture in the new hostel block0.21 (far)
The first sentence shares almost no words with the question, but it means the same thing. The last one shares the word "placement" but means something else. Embeddings get this right; keyword search does not.
Famous example With word embeddings you can do arithmetic on meaning: king − man + woman ≈ queen. The direction "man → woman" is roughly the same as "king → queen".

Where embeddings are used: semantic search, recommendations ("students who liked this course also liked..."), finding duplicate questions, and RAG, which we build in Session 4.

# Compare sentence meanings (run in Google Colab)
!pip install sentence-transformers
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
q = "How do I apply for placements?"
faqs = ["What is the process for campus recruitment?",
        "Placement of furniture in the new hostel block"]

scores = util.cos_sim(model.encode(q), model.encode(faqs))
for faq, s in zip(faqs, scores[0]):
    print(f"{s:.2f}  {faq}")

6. Context window

The context window is the maximum number of tokens a model can look at in one go. It includes everything: the instructions, the whole conversation so far, any documents you pasted, and the answer it's writing.

Analogy: an open-book exam with a small desk You can bring notes, but your desk only fits a certain number of pages. If you add more, the oldest pages fall off. The model can only "see" what's on the desk right now.
  • Sizes vary a lot: from a few thousand tokens in small models to hundreds of thousands, or even a million, in large ones. Check each model's documentation.
  • When a chat gets longer than the window, the app drops or summarises older messages. That's why a chatbot sometimes "forgets" what you said at the start.
  • LLMs have no memory between calls. Every request starts fresh. Chat apps feel like they remember because they re-send the whole conversation every time. You'll build this yourself in Session 3.
  • Bigger isn't free: more tokens means higher cost and slower answers. Models can also miss details buried in the middle of very long inputs.

7. Temperature

Remember that the model gives a probability for every possible next token. Temperature controls how it picks from that list.

  • Low temperature (0 – 0.3): almost always picks the top choice. Predictable and consistent.
  • High temperature (0.8 – 1.5): gives less likely words a real chance. Creative and varied, but more likely to go off track.
Try it: move the slider
TaskTemperatureWhy
Answer from the syllabus, extract data to JSON, write codeLow (0 – 0.3)You want the same correct answer every time.
General chat, explanationsMedium (0.5 – 0.8)Natural but still focused.
Names for the college tech fest, poems, brainstormingHigh (0.9 – 1.2)You want variety and surprise.

You may also see top-p. It's a related setting: "only pick from the smallest group of words that together cover p% of the probability". Usually you tune one of the two, not both.

8. Why LLMs hallucinate

A hallucination is when a model gives an answer that sounds confident and fluent but is false or made up.

Example

Prompt: "Summarise the 2019 research paper 'Quantum Biryani Optimization' by Dr. R. Sharma, IIT Bombay."

This paper doesn't exist. A model may still write a detailed summary, with methods and results, because that's what a summary of a paper looks like. Other common cases: fake citations, wrong dates, invented court cases, made-up library functions in code.

Why it happens

  1. It predicts plausible text, not true text. Its goal is "what words usually come next", not "what is a fact".
  2. It has no built-in fact database. What it learned is stored as patterns in its parameters, not as a list of facts it can check.
  3. Knowledge cutoff. It only knows what was in its training data up to a certain date. It doesn't know your college's latest timetable.
  4. It is rewarded for answering. Training often rewards a helpful-looking answer more than "I don't know", so it guesses.

How to reduce it

  • Give it the source: paste the document, or use RAG to fetch the right text automatically (Session 4).
  • Allow "I don't know": add "If the answer isn't in the text, say you don't know."
  • Ask for citations, then actually check them.
  • Lower the temperature for factual tasks.
  • Give it tools like web search or a calculator (Day 2: agents).
  • Keep a human in the loop for anything important: marks, money, medical, legal.

9. Open vs closed models, and how to choose

Closed modelsOpen-weight models
ExamplesGPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google)Llama (Meta), Mistral, also Gemma (Google), Qwen (Alibaba), DeepSeek
How you use itThrough the company's app or API. You can't download the model.Download and run it yourself: on a laptop (e.g. with Ollama), your own server, or a host like Groq.
StrengthsUsually the most capable. No hardware needed. Quick to start.Full control. Data can stay on your machines. Can be customised (fine-tuned). No per-token fee if self-hosted.
WeaknessesPay per token. Your data goes to the provider. The provider can change or retire the model.Need GPUs and skills to run well. Big models are expensive to host. Licences vary.

"Open-weight" means the trained model is published. It doesn't always mean the training data and code are open too, and licences can restrict commercial use. Always read the licence.

How to choose: a simple checklist

  1. Quality: is it good enough at your task? Test with 10–20 real examples.
  2. Cost: how many requests per day × tokens per request × price per token?
  3. Speed: does the user need an answer in under a second (chat) or can it wait (overnight report)?
  4. Privacy: can this data leave your organisation?
  5. Language: does it handle Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, etc. well?
  6. Context length: do you need to fit long documents?
  7. Licence: are you allowed to use it commercially?
Scenarios: which would you pick?
24-hour hackathon prototypeA closed model through an API, or a free tier (Gemini free tier, Groq). Fastest to start; quality matters more than cost here.
Hospital summarising patient records; data must not leave the buildingAn open-weight model running on the hospital's own servers.
Classifying 10 lakh customer reviews a day as positive / negativeA small, cheap, fast model. A giant model is overkill for a simple task at high volume.
Customer support chatbot in KannadaTest several models on real Kannada questions before choosing. Language quality varies a lot.
Practical tip Start with the strongest model you can access to prove the idea works. Then try smaller, cheaper models and keep the smallest one that's still good enough.

10. Recap

  1. AI ⊃ ML ⊃ Deep Learning ⊃ LLMs. ML learns rules from examples.
  2. Transformers use attention to understand each word using the words around it.
  3. An LLM predicts the next token, again and again. Training stages turn it into an assistant.
  4. Tokens are the unit of input, cost and limits. Embeddings turn meaning into numbers.
  5. The context window is the model's short-term memory. LLMs don't remember between calls.
  6. Temperature: low for facts, high for creativity.
  7. LLMs hallucinate because they generate plausible text. Ground them with sources.
  8. Choose models by quality, cost, speed, privacy, language and licence, not by hype.

Quick quiz

1. An ML spam filter is given 10,000 labelled emails. What does it produce?A model: rules/patterns it learned to decide whether a new email is spam.
2. What does "attention" help a transformer do?Decide which other words in the input matter for understanding each word (e.g. "bank" near "money" vs near "river").
3. Roughly how many tokens is a 750-word English essay?About 1,000 tokens (1 token ≈ ¾ word).
4. Why does a chatbot "forget" the start of a very long conversation?The conversation exceeded the context window, so older messages were dropped or summarised.
5. You're extracting student names and marks into JSON. High or low temperature?Low (around 0). You want consistent, exact output.
6. Give two reasons LLMs hallucinate and one way to reduce it.They predict plausible text, not verified facts; they have a knowledge cutoff; training rewards answering. Reduce it by giving source documents (RAG), allowing "I don't know", lowering temperature, or checking citations.
7. A bank wants an internal assistant, and customer data can't leave its servers. Open or closed model?An open-weight model hosted on the bank's own infrastructure (or a closed model only if the provider offers a deployment that meets the bank's data rules).
